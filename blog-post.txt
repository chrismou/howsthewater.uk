<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>tl;dr</strong> I built <a href="https://howsthewater.uk">howsthewater.uk</a>, a free web app that tracks live storm overflow spills, river levels, and flood warnings for popular swimming, paddling, and fishing spots across Yorkshire, the Thames Valley, and the North West. It pulls in open data from water companies and the Environment Agency, works out which sewage overflows are genuinely upstream of where you're standing, and tells you what the monitors say—without ever making dodgy claims about whether the water is "safe". It's built with PHP 8.5 and Laravel, and the code is on GitHub.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><a href="https://howsthewater.uk"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1024" height="576" src="https://api.mou.me/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/howsthewater-home.png" alt="How's the Water homepage showing river overviews, live overflow statuses, and gauge readings" class="wp-image-0000" /></a><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><strong>[SCREENSHOT: The How's the Water homepage showing the Yorkshire region summary, featured spots, and river cards]</strong></figcaption></figure>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">A quick dip in the Wharfe</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Living in Leeds, we're pretty spoiled for access to Yorkshire's rivers. If you've spent any warm summer weekend around Otley, Ilkley, or Burnsall, you'll know how popular wild swimming and paddleboarding have become over the last few years. But alongside that surge in enthusiasm, there's been an equally massive surge in public awareness around storm overflows—what the water companies politely call Event Duration Monitoring (EDM), and what everyone else calls dumping sewage into rivers.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Every time friends or family talked about heading out for a swim or taking the kids down to the river, the same conversation played out: <em>"It rained yesterday... did the sewage works overflow? Are the pipes spilling right now? How high is the river after the rain?"</em></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The frustrating thing is that the raw data actually exists. Under public and regulatory pressure, water companies (Yorkshire Water, Thames Water, United Utilities) now publish near real-time telemetry through an open data initiative called Stream. Meanwhile, the Environment Agency provides real-time APIs for river gauges, rainfall, and flood warnings.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The catch? It's scattered across separate corporate GIS maps, clunky open-data portals, and raw data feeds that are about as far from a quick mobile check on a riverbank as you can get. So my dev brain kicked in: <em>"I can stitch these APIs together and build a clean little dashboard over a weekend."</em></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Spoiler: it was not a weekend project.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The straight-line fallacy</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When you start building a location app, the obvious shortcut is to draw a circle on a map. Drop a pin at your swimming spot in Otley, look for any sewage overflow monitor within a 5 km radius, and report what they're doing.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Except rivers don't work as the crow flies.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If an overflow pipe is 400 metres away from where you're standing, but it sits <em>downstream</em> of the weir, that water is heading away from you. You could swim around all afternoon and never come into contact with it. On the other hand, if an overflow is sitting 12 kilometres away <em>upstream</em> on a little tributary beck, every drop it discharges is heading straight down the river to where you're standing.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To give people an answer they can actually use, you have to ignore straight lines completely and follow the water. That means finding the river's actual physical path and measuring distances along the flow.</p>

<figure class="wp-block-image size-large"><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1024" height="512" src="https://api.mou.me/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/howsthewater-spot-map.png" alt="Spot map on How's the Water showing upstream monitors along the river centreline" class="wp-image-0000" /><figcaption class="wp-element-caption"><strong>[SCREENSHOT: Spot page map for Otley or Ilkley showing upstream monitors positioned along the river path]</strong></figcaption></figure>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Untangling the Ordnance Survey (with a little help from Claude)</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To calculate distances along a river, you need vector data for the rivers themselves. My first attempt was pulling river lines from OpenStreetMap via the Overpass API. But Overpass queries for winding, hundred-mile rivers are notoriously slow, prone to timing out, and frequently tell you to go away.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead, I turned to the Ordnance Survey Open Rivers dataset: an official, highly detailed 50 MB GeoPackage containing vector links for every named watercourse across Great Britain. It sounded ideal. It was, however, where the real head-scratching began.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Working with raw UK hydrological data introduces you to some wonderful real-world mapping quirks:</p>

<ul class="wp-block-list">
  <li><strong>Names repeat everywhere:</strong> There isn't just one River Calder or River Derwent in Britain; there are several. Yorkshire alone is littered with dozens of separate becks sharing identical names, and Cumbria casually has two completely different River Gretas. If you query for "River Calder" without context, you get bits and pieces scattered across Yorkshire, Lancashire, and Scotland.</li>
  <li><strong>Lakes and reservoirs break the lines:</strong> Rivers aren't continuous lines in GIS databases. When a river flows into a reservoir, the line often vanishes and picks up again on the other side, sometimes under a slightly different name.</li>
  <li><strong>Flow directions are sometimes backwards:</strong> OS links include a recorded flow direction. But around islands, old mill cuts, and weirs, whoever originally mapped the section often drew the line in the wrong direction. If your code tries to follow the arrows downstream, it suddenly hits a dead end after 50 metres in the middle of a field.</li>
</ul>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The breakthrough was realising I couldn't just follow the links in order. I had to treat the river sections as an unorganised web—an undirected graph. The plan was: seed an approximate location for where the river ends (its mouth), find the nearest watercourse link, and run Dijkstra's algorithm backwards across the network to find the point furthest away (the true source). Once you have the source and the mouth, you can trace the shortest path between them to get a clean main river stem, and then bridge across gaps like reservoirs.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Once I'd mapped out that strategy, I didn't fancy spending days wrestling with priority queues, graph-traversal edge cases, and binary geometry decoding in PHP. So I specced out the requirements and handed the heavy lifting of the code over to Claude, which wrote the core of <a href="https://github.com/chrismou/howsthewater.uk/blob/main/app/Services/OrdnanceSurvey/OpenRiversReader.php"><code>OpenRiversReader</code></a>. It did an amazing job with the geometry maths, leaving me free to get on with building the actual app.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">With the river converted into a single, clean polyline from source to mouth, the app can snap any swimming spot, overflow monitor, or gauge onto the line and calculate its exact position in "river metres".</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Walking the tributary tree</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Of course, rivers aren't just single lines; they're trees of tributaries. If you're standing at Otley on the Wharfe, you don't just care about spills directly on the Wharfe. You also care about spills on every tributary—becks, gills, and brooks—that joins the Wharfe upstream of Otley.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Our <a href="https://github.com/chrismou/howsthewater.uk/blob/main/app/Services/Conditions/UpstreamMonitorFinder.php"><code>UpstreamMonitorFinder</code></a> handles this recursively. When a tributary is imported, we record exactly where it joins the parent river (its <code>joins_at_metres</code>). When querying upstream monitors for a spot, it grabs all monitors on the main river above the spot, and then walks up every tributary that joins above that point:</p>

<pre class="wp-block-code"><code>// Calculate flow distance down the tributary and along the main river
foreach ($tributaries as $tributary) {
    $monitors = $monitors->concat($this->collect(
        $tributary,
        $tributary->length_metres,
        $offsetMetres + ($belowMetres - $tributary->joins_at_metres),
    ));
}</code></pre>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Because distances compound as they flow down the network, the output is sorted by actual water distance: <em>"Monitor A spilled 2 miles upstream on the Wharfe; Monitor B spilled 4 miles upstream on Cock Beck."</em></p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Water company feeds have no memory</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The next hurdle was the overflow data itself. Water companies publish their live feeds as ArcGIS feature services. They work fine, but they only tell you the <em>current</em> state of each monitor: <em>"Is this overflow spilling right this second? Yes or No."</em></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There is no history endpoint. If an overflow dumped raw sewage for six hours overnight while you were asleep, by 8:00 AM the feed simply reports <code>Not discharging</code>. Unless you were staring at the feed at 3:00 AM, that spill never happened as far as the data is concerned.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To fix this, a scheduled background job polls the feeds every 15 minutes. It watches for status changes, opens a spill event record when a monitor starts discharging, updates the duration as it continues, and closes the event when it stops. Over time, this builds an independent 30-day spill history for each spot: how many minutes it spilled each day, and when the last spill actually finished.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Knowing when not to trust the numbers</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If you read my <a href="/blog/building-a-dexcom-powered-glucose-monitor-with-a-raspbery-pi-pico">earlier post about building a Dexcom glucose monitor</a>, you'll know I have strong feelings about stale data. Showing a reassuring number on a screen when that number is secretly hours out of date is worse than showing nothing at all.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The exact same problem exists with river monitors. What happens if an overflow monitor loses its mobile signal on a rainy riverbank, or a water company feed goes down? A naive app would look at the missing data, find zero active spills, and cheerfully put up a big green <em>"No spills detected!"</em> badge. Someone glances at their phone, jumps in the river, and ends up poorly.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We designed How's the Water around one hard rule: <strong>the absence of data is never presented as clean water</strong>.</p>

<ul class="wp-block-list">
  <li>If an upstream monitor is reporting offline, it is explicitly counted and displayed as offline.</li>
  <li>If <em>every</em> upstream monitor is offline, the overall spill status flips to <code>Offline</code>—with a neutral grey badge, never green.</li>
  <li>If a feed or river level gauge hasn't delivered a reading in over six hours, the site flags it as stale and shows a warning.</li>
  <li>Every page reinforces the distinction: we report what the monitors say; we do not say the water is safe or clean.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Rounding out the picture: rain, levels, and floods</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sewage overflows are only half the story. If a river is in raging flood after heavy rain, you shouldn't be swimming or paddling in it anyway. So the app pulls in three other pieces of Environment Agency data:</p>

<ol class="wp-block-list">
  <li><strong>River Levels:</strong> Every spot links to its nearest Environment Agency level gauge, preferring gauges that measure height above the riverbed in metres (which makes intuitive sense) over gauges that report metres Above Ordnance Datum (mAOD), which tell you you're 42.6 metres above sea level—not terribly helpful when trying to work out if the river is over the bank.</li>
  <li><strong>Rainfall:</strong> We link the nearest EA tipping-bucket rain gauge within 20 km and sum up the 24-hour and 48-hour totals. If an upstream overflow hasn't reported a spill yet, but 35 mm of rain has fallen on the hills upstream in the last 12 hours, you can make a sensible guess about what's coming.</li>
  <li><strong>Flood Warnings:</strong> Every spot is matched against Environment Agency flood alert and warning areas. If an active flood warning covers your stretch of water, a warning banner appears across the site.</li>
</ol>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We also added coastal beaches and designated lake bathing sites. Because the sea and lakes don't have an "upstream", they look at storm overflows within a 3 km radius instead, alongside official Environment Agency bathing water classifications, weekly water samples, and daily pollution risk forecasts.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Under the hood</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The stack is PHP 8.5 on Laravel 12, MySQL 8, with Blade, Tailwind CSS, and Leaflet on the frontend. Data ingest runs on scheduled background Artisan commands every 15 minutes, which means web visitors and API consumers never wait on third-party API calls.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One neat technical problem came up with caching. All computed conditions for spots, rivers, and regions are cached as typed objects (DTOs). But if you cache serialized PHP objects and then deploy a code update that adds or modifies a property on that class, PHP will choke with <code>__PHP_Incomplete_Class</code> errors until the cache is cleared.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To avoid running a blunt cache-clear command on deploy that would leave every visitor hitting cold database queries, our <a href="https://github.com/chrismou/howsthewater.uk/blob/main/app/Services/Conditions/ConditionsCache.php"><code>ConditionsCache</code></a> generates a short hash based on the MD5 checksums of the DTO PHP source files themselves:</p>

<pre class="wp-block-code"><code>public static function codeVersion(): string
{
    if (self::$codeVersion !== null) {
        return self::$codeVersion;
    }

    $sources = array_map(
        fn (string $class) => md5_file((new ReflectionClass($class))->getFileName()) ?: $class,
        config('cache.serializable_classes', []),
    );

    return self::$codeVersion = substr(md5(implode('', $sources)), 0, 8);
}</code></pre>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Every cache key includes this hash. When a deployment changes the shape of a class, the cache key namespace automatically shifts, so the new release never tries to read old objects. Ingest commands mark regions as changed, and a background task warms the new cache entries in place. Fast pages, zero downtime, and no serialization crashes.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There are also auto-generated RSS feeds for every river and spot (e.g. <code>/yorkshire/wharfe/otley/feed</code>) that post an update whenever an upstream overflow starts or stops, plus a read-only JSON API at <code>/api/v1</code> if you want to build your own scripts.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Where it stands</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The site is live right now at <a href="https://howsthewater.uk">howsthewater.uk</a>, currently covering three major regions:</p>

<ul class="wp-block-list">
  <li><strong>Yorkshire:</strong> The Wharfe, Aire, Calder, Don, Nidd, Ure, Swale, Derwent, Ouse, Esk, and Hull, plus all their becks.</li>
  <li><strong>Thames Valley:</strong> The non-tidal Thames from its source down to Teddington Lock, including 24 major tributaries (the Windrush, Cherwell, Kennet, Mole, Wey, Colne, etc.).</li>
  <li><strong>North West:</strong> The Ribble, Lune, Kent, Eden, and Cumbrian Derwent.</li>
</ul>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The entire codebase is open source under the MIT licence on GitHub: <a href="https://github.com/chrismou/howsthewater.uk">github.com/chrismou/howsthewater.uk</a>.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I've been dogfooding it ahead of weekend trips out to the river. The plan from here is to expand into more regions as time allows (South West Water and Scottish Water format their feeds a bit differently, which will be another fun puzzle), and maybe add push notifications or email alerts for specific spots.</p>
